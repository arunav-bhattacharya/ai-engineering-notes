Models come and go; *better, cheaper, faster* is forever. A great model that's too slow loses users (a next-day stock predictor that takes two days is worthless), and one that's too expensive loses its ROI. Optimization happens at three levels — **model** (smaller models, better architectures), **hardware** (better chips), and **service** (running models on hardware efficiently against real traffic) — making this one of AI's most interdisciplinary corners. Even if you only ever call APIs, understanding these techniques lets you evaluate providers and diagnose latency/cost pain.

## Understanding Inference Optimization

In production, an **inference server** runs models on hardware; the surrounding **inference service** receives, routes, and preprocesses requests (model APIs *are* inference services — self-hosting means building your own).

### Computational bottlenecks

<Figure num="9.1" caption="Two bottleneck types, one memory hierarchy — and the prefill/decode split that shapes everything downstream.">
  <Ch09Bottlenecks />
</Figure>

Optimization starts with knowing what you're limited by:

- **Compute-bound** work is limited by arithmetic capacity (think password cracking; image-generation inference).
- **Memory bandwidth-bound** work is limited by data movement (loading weights from memory to compute units). The classification comes from *arithmetic intensity* — operations per byte of memory access — visualized in roofline charts by profiling tools. (Terminology trap: "memory-bound" sometimes means bandwidth-bound, sometimes memory-*capacity*-bound — the OOM kind — and capacity problems usually reduce to bandwidth problems anyway, since you can split work if transfer is fast enough.)

For transformer LLMs, the split maps exactly onto inference's two phases: **prefill** (input tokens processed in parallel — compute-bound) and **decode** (one token at a time, each requiring the weights to stream through compute units — bandwidth-bound). Different profiles, different cures: more FLOP/s for compute-bound, more bandwidth or fewer bytes for bandwidth-bound — and production systems often separate the two phases onto different machines. Most LLM workloads today are bandwidth-bound.

### Online and batch APIs

Providers split offerings into **online APIs** (latency-first, process on arrival) and **batch APIs** (cost-first — roughly half price for hours-scale turnaround), the latter fitting synthetic data generation, periodic reports, onboarding document crunches, model-migration reprocessing, newsletter generation, and reindexing. (Distinct from classic ML "batch inference," which *pre*-computes predictions — impossible when prompts are open-ended.) Online APIs still micro-batch when latency allows, and most offer **streaming** — tokens as generated, slashing waiting time at the cost of showing responses before they can be fully vetted.

### Inference performance metrics

<Figure num="9.2" caption="Latency decomposes into TTFT + TPOT; throughput converts to cost; goodput keeps throughput honest; MFU/MBU measure real efficiency.">
  <Ch09Metrics />
</Figure>

- **Latency** = **TTFT** (time to first token — the prefill phase, scaling with input length, expectation varying by app) + **TPOT** (time per output token) × output length. (~120ms/token matches very fast reading — enough for streaming chat.) Same total latency, different feels: instant-first-token-slow-stream versus the reverse is a UX decision, tunable by shifting compute between prefill and decode. Two gotchas: user-visible TTFT ≠ model TTFT when hidden steps run (CoT, agent plans — hence "time to publish"), and **latency is a distribution** — one 3-second outlier turns ten ~100ms requests into a misleading 390ms average; report p50/p90/p99 and investigate outliers.
- **Throughput** — output tokens/s across all users (count input and output separately; prefill and decode differ) or requests/minute — converts directly to cost ($2/h at 100 tokens/s ≈ $5.56 per million output tokens). Comparisons across models are fuzzy (different tokenizers!) — prefer cost-per-request. And mind the **latency/throughput trade-off**: production teams report 2–3× throughput is available if you'll sacrifice TTFT/TPOT — which is why **goodput** exists: requests per second *meeting your SLO*. Completing 100/min with 30 in-SLO is goodput 30; optimizing raw throughput can quietly destroy the experience.
- **Utilization**, measured right: the famous `nvidia-smi` "GPU utilization" only measures *busy time* — a chip doing 1 of its possible 100 ops/s reports 100%. The metrics with meaning are **MFU** (achieved throughput ÷ theoretical peak FLOP/s throughput) and **MBU** (bandwidth used ÷ peak: params × bytes/param × tokens/s over peak bandwidth — a 7B FP16 model at 100 tokens/s uses 1.4 TB/s; on a 2 TB/s chip that's 70%). Training MFU >50% is good; inference runs lower (prefill higher than decode). The point isn't maximal utilization — it's getting jobs done faster and cheaper.

### AI accelerators

Hardware and AI co-evolved (insufficient compute helped freeze AI funding in the 1970s; GPUs made the 2012 deep-learning revival possible by replacing thousands of CPUs with a couple of cards). **GPUs** dominate: where CPUs offer a few powerful cores for sequential work, GPUs offer thousands of small cores for parallel work — and matrix multiplication, the bulk of neural-net FLOPs, parallelizes perfectly. The success spawned a zoo of accelerators (TPUs and many vendor-specific designs), with a notable trend toward **inference-specialized chips** (inference can reach ~90% of deployed ML cost): optimized for low precision and fast memory access over training's huge memory demands; edge chips follow the same logic. Chips also differ in compute primitives (scalar/vector/tensor units) — architecture and hardware shape each other (the transformer was originally designed to run fast on TPUs).

Three characteristics matter across use cases: **computational capability** (peak FLOP/s — roughly doubling as precision halves; treat advertised peaks skeptically), **memory size and bandwidth** across the three-tier hierarchy (CPU DRAM: big and slow ~25–50 GB/s → GPU HBM: 24–80 GB at 256 GB/s–1.5+ TB/s → on-chip SRAM: ≤~40 MB at 10+ TB/s — most GPU optimization is hierarchy management, which mainstream frameworks barely expose, hence the rise of kernel languages), and **power** (a flagship GPU at peak for a year ≈ 70% of a US household's annual electricity — the data-center energy story from Chapter 2 again). Selection questions: can it run the workload, how fast, at what price — answered by FLOP/s, memory size, and bandwidth.

## Model-Level Optimization

<Figure num="9.3" caption="Three transformer cost centers — size, autoregressive decoding, attention — and the technique families attacking each.">
  <Ch09ModelOpt />
</Figure>

Model-level techniques modify the model (and may change behavior — benchmarks show the *same* model varying in quality across inference providers because of optimization choices; evaluate quality, not just speed). Three cost centers:

### Model compression

**Quantization** (Chapter 7) is the workhorse — easy, general, halving memory per precision halving, near its floor at ~1 bit. **Distillation** (Chapter 8) captures a big model's behavior in fewer parameters. **Pruning** — either removing parts of the architecture or zeroing low-value parameters for sparsity — shows dramatic research results (90%+ of nonzero parameters removable without accuracy loss in some networks) but remains rare in practice: harder to apply, smaller wins, and hardware support for sparsity is spotty.

### Overcoming the autoregressive decoding bottleneck

One token at a time is slow *and* expensive (output tokens cost ~2–4× input tokens; one output token impacts latency like ~100 input tokens). Three attack vectors:

- **Speculative decoding**: a fast *draft* model proposes K tokens; the *target* model verifies all K **in parallel** (verification has prefill's parallel profile — and decode's idle FLOPs make it nearly free); accept the agreeing prefix, generate one real token, repeat. Easy tokens are predictable, so acceptance runs high (higher still for structured text like code). Result: roughly *halved latency with unchanged quality* (a 4B draft sped a 70B target ~8× per draft token), ~50 lines of code to implement, now standard in major serving frameworks.
- **Inference with reference**: same idea, draft tokens copied **from the input** instead of from a model — perfect when outputs heavily quote context (document Q&A, bug-fixing, multi-turn chat); ~2× speedups there, no extra model, useless elsewhere.
- **Parallel decoding**: generate several future tokens *simultaneously* (you can often guess "the" two tokens ahead), via the same decoder (Lookahead, with Jacobi-style iterative verification — regenerate only the failed tokens) or extra trained decoding heads (Medusa, with tree-based selection among each head's candidates; reported up to 1.9× token-generation gains). Powerful, less intuitive, harder to implement.

### Attention optimization and the KV cache

Decoding token x<sub>t+1</sub> needs key/value vectors for everything before it — recomputing them every step would be madness, so the **KV cache** stores them (inference-only; training sees all tokens at once). The catch: cache size = 2 × batch × sequence length × layers × model dimension × bytes — ~54 GB for a 13B-class model at batch 32 and 2K context, and ~3 TB (3× the weights!) for a 500B model at batch 512 — *the* long-context bottleneck. Three technique families:

- **Redesign attention** (training-time changes): local windowed attention (attend to a nearby window, optionally interleaved with global layers), **multi-query** and **grouped-query attention** (share KV pairs across query heads, or per group), cross-layer attention (share KV across adjacent layers). A production chat company stacking three of these cut KV cache 20× — turning memory from their binding constraint into a non-issue.
- **Manage the cache**: vLLM's **PagedAttention** (non-contiguous blocks à la virtual memory — less fragmentation, flexible sharing), KV quantization, adaptive compression, selective retention.
- **Write kernels**: hardware-specialized code for hot operations — **FlashAttention** fused transformer operations for dramatic speedups (and gets rewritten per GPU generation). The general kernel toolkit: vectorization, parallelization, loop tiling (cache-aware access order), operator fusion (skip redundant memory trips). Kernels live below Python — in CUDA, Triton, ROCm — and **compilers** (torch.compile, XLA, TensorRT, TVM) *lower* model code onto hardware, swapping in optimized kernels as they go. A published stacking exercise on a 7B model — compile → INT8 → INT4 → speculative decoding — multiplied throughput severalfold, with the telling caveat that quality impact went unmeasured.

## Service-Level Optimization

<Figure num="9.4" caption="The service-level playbook — resource management that leaves the model (and its quality) untouched.">
  <Ch09Service />
</Figure>

Service-level techniques manage resources without touching the model — no quality risk:

- **Batching** — the bus instead of private cars. **Static**: depart when full (everyone waits for the last passenger). **Dynamic**: full *or* schedule (bounded latency, occasionally half-empty buses). **Continuous/in-flight** (the Orca insight): return each finished response immediately and slot a new request into its place — short responses stop waiting for 1,000-token batchmates.
- **Decouple prefill and decode** onto separate instances: colocated, an arriving prefill job drains compute from in-flight decodes, hurting everyone's TPOT. Disaggregation (with intermediate states shipped over fast interconnects) significantly raises in-SLO volume; tune the prefill:decode instance ratio to your workload (long inputs + TTFT priority → 2:1–4:1; the reverse → 1:2–1:1).
- **Prompt caching** (a.k.a. prefix/context caching): process shared prompt segments once — system prompts above all, but also long documents queried repeatedly and earlier conversation turns. A 1K-token system prompt at a million daily calls ≈ a *billion* repeated tokens avoided; providers offer it with steep cached-token discounts (up to ~90% cost and ~75% latency reductions on long-context patterns), priced against cache storage. Self-hosting it takes real engineering.
- **Parallelism**: **replica** (more model copies — simple, scales traffic, and packing different model sizes onto different GPUs is a bin-packing puzzle), **tensor/intra-operator** (split each operation's matrices across devices — both fits models too big for one machine *and* cuts latency, minus communication overhead), **pipeline** (stage layers across machines — throughput up, per-request latency up; serving avoids it when latency matters, training embraces it), plus long-input specialists (**context parallelism**: split the input across devices; **sequence parallelism**: split operators across devices).

Choosing among all of the above is workload arithmetic: KV-cache work matters most for long contexts; prompt caching for shared prefixes and multi-turn; replica scaling when latency outranks cost. The chapter's closing shortlist of highest-impact techniques across use cases: **quantization, tensor parallelism, replica parallelism, and attention optimization**.
